* Overview

Just across the Missouri River from Omaha, in downtown Council Bluffs, sits the
Pottawattamie County Squirrel Cage Jail. It was built in 1885, held prisoners
until 1969, and is the only three-story rotary jail ever made. Only three rotary
jails survive today: this one, a one-story jail in Gallatin, Missouri, and a
two-story jail in Crawfordsville, Indiana. The Historical Society of
Pottawattamie County now runs the Council Bluffs jail as a museum.

If you've never heard of a rotary jail, the idea is simple and a little absurd.
William H. Brown and Benjamin F. Haugh patented the design in 1881: instead of
lining cells up along a corridor, you put the inmates in pie-shaped cells inside
a large iron drum and spin it. Each floor has a single opening, and the jailer
turns a hand crank until the cell he wants lines up with it. That way, one guard
can control every prisoner in the building without ever stepping into the
cellblock. The drum in Council Bluffs holds three tiers of ten cells each and
weighs about forty-five tons.

#+caption: Model and floor plan of the rotary jail
#+attr_html: :alt An acrylic model and patent schematic showing the pie-shaped cells arranged in a circle around a central spindle inside the octagonal outer cage
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/jail-model.webp]]

It didn't take long for the problems to show up. Any inmate with an arm or leg
through the bars while the drum turned would have it crushed against the outer
cage, and some inmates started doing it on purpose. A broken arm got you moved
to the infirmary, and the infirmary only had a wooden ceiling between you and
the outside, so a few of them broke through it and shimmied down the drainpipes
to escape.

Over the years, the weight of the drum caused the central bearings and shafts to
sag until the crank was nearly impossible to turn. Fire was an even bigger
worry, since the only way to get everyone out was to rotate the drum one cell at
a time past the single exit.

* The Experience

From the outside, 226 Pearl Street looks like any other Victorian brick building
downtown. You walk in through the booking room, and right away you're standing
next to the jail's only solitary cell. It isn't really a room at all - it's the
gap between two steel doors that separate the booking room from a narrow
hallway. Once both doors were shut, the inmate had a solid steel door at their
back and a barred one in front of them, with barely enough space for a modern
adult to fit. You can't sit down in there, and you can hardly bend your knees.

#+caption: The solitary confinement corridor
#+attr_html: :alt A narrow, dim steel hallway lit by hanging incandescent bulbs, with a barred steel door on the left and vertical iron bars on the right
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/corridor.webp]]

The hallway on the other side runs straight into the first-floor cell area. It
was a shortcut, letting jailers get a rowdy inmate locked up quickly without
going through all the normal cell doors.

Once you're in the cell room, the drum takes up most of the space. The hand
crank and gears that turned it are still on the first floor, and it's hard to
picture one person moving all of that iron by hand. The cells are narrow wedges
that shrink toward the center, and standing in one, there isn't a single
parallel wall or a window in sight.

#+caption: Inside a wedge-shaped cell
#+attr_html: :alt Interior of a pie-slice cell through iron bars, showing narrow tapering steel walls, two bunk beds, and a single ceiling bulb
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/pie-cell.webp]]

Make sure to look up while you're in there. The steel ceiling is covered in
names and dates that inmates scratched and painted over the decades, and it's
all still there.

#+caption: Inmate graffiti etched into the steel ceiling
#+attr_html: :alt Riveted steel ceiling covered in names and dates carved and painted by prisoners across the mid-twentieth century
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/graffiti.webp]]

The first floor is also where inmates showered, if you can call it that. The
shower was a bare pipe sticking out of the wall in a wide open space, with no
privacy at all.

Upstairs on the second floor, there's a drop hatch in the floor. Getting down to
the first floor the proper way meant unlocking and relocking door after door,
which took far too long when a fight broke out below. Instead, guards would
unlock the hatch and jump straight down.

At the top of the building, you can look straight down onto the top of the drum.

#+caption: The central pivot axle and radial cage structure from above
#+attr_html: :alt Looking down at the massive circular iron framework and central riveted steel pivot beam supporting the rotating cell drum
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/rotary-mechanism.webp]]

This view explains one of the jail's worst problems. Each cell's toilet sat at
the center of the cylinder, where there was almost no ventilation, so the smell
drifted straight up the drum and into the apartment where the jailer lived with
his family.

#+caption: The fourth-floor family quarters
#+attr_html: :alt A vintage bathroom with green peeling walls, clawfoot tub, patterned rug, and lace curtains over an arched window overlooking downtown Council Bluffs
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/quarters-bathroom.webp]]

The apartment itself is a strange contrast to everything below it. It has a
clawfoot tub, lace curtains, and arched windows looking out over downtown, all
one flight of stairs away from the cells and their smell.

Throughout the building, there are reminders of who was actually held here.

#+caption: Daily inmate tally slate
#+attr_html: :alt A slate chalkboard hanging against a white brick wall listing handwritten chalk counts for bullpen, holdover, hospital, women, and trusties totaling 44
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261028-detour-001-squirrel-cage-jail/tally-slate.webp]]

A chalk slate tallies the day's count by where each inmate was held: bullpen,
holdover, hospital, women, and trusties. The jail also had a children's wing,
which held children as young as infants. Most of them were there because the
jail doubled as child services at the time, but some were held as genuine
"criminals." One display case even holds real rope from the nooses used to hang
some of the jail's prisoners.

Local tourism leans heavily on ghost stories and paranormal tours, but you don't
need any of that here. The building is strange enough on its own.

* Going

The jail is in downtown Council Bluffs, a couple of minutes off the I-80 / I-29
interchange and about ten minutes from downtown Omaha. You can park on Pearl
Street right out front or in the small lot next door.

Tours are self-guided, but there are docents throughout the building who can
explain the drum, the infirmary, and the family quarters. Plan on 45 minutes to
an hour. If you're driving through Omaha or Council Bluffs, or anywhere along
I-80, it's worth the stop.
