* Overview

What is a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liminal_space][liminal space]]?

If you look online, you'll find hallways, empty pools, or malls with the lights
off. You scroll through and experience the same eerie feeling each time: "This
feels off. It feels like something is wrong, but I can't pin it down."

The feeling is real and the good news is that you're not alone. There's a real
reason these places do that to you, and it turns out the word we all use for
them is describing something else entirely. I've felt a strong connection with
liminal spaces my whole life and I wanted to sit down here and write about it
for a while, similar to [[https://cleberg.net/blog/the-night-feeling.html][The Night Feeling]], which I wrote recently.

* Where It Happens

A hotel hallway at 3 AM.

A bus station lounge between connections.

A parking ramp stairwell, lit by a lone sodium lamp, flooded with yellow light.

#+caption: An empty hallway after hours
#+attr_html: :alt Empty interior corridor at night with a brick wall on the left, recessed ceiling lights, a speckled floor, and a single wooden door at the far end
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20260913-nowhere-specifically/liminal.webp]]

Imagine yourself standing in one of these locations. Look around and picture the
carpet, the silence or the sounds of strangers somewhere in the distance, the
light and shadows bouncing off the walls. Nothing is happening. Nothing was ever
supposed to happen here; the room was built for you to walk through it, and yet
you stopped.

I often find myself standing on an empty street, meant to be bustling with
traffic, looking around at the pure absence as I exist in that space by myself.
The lamps shine down, flickering and glowing yellow. The bugs chirp and make
sounds throughout, letting you know that there are hundreds or thousands of
creatures around you, but it feels empty nonetheless.

* The Feeling

It's not fear, or loneliness, or nostalgia. You're simply somewhere you have no
reason to be, at a time nobody intended for you to be there. Or you're in a
place you'll never think of again. Or a place where you know no one has ever
stood still.

It's strange, because these are the same conditions I wrote about loving at
night: empty space, no one asking anything of me. At night that emptiness felt
like permission to be alone in my own space, but here it feels like a mistake.

* The Effect

These spaces are built to move people through them, accounting for the nonstop
movement of crowds. Take the crowds out of the picture and suddenly the room is
useless. The airport terminal without people is a room without a purpose. When
you stumble upon one of these rooms, this is where you encounter that uneasy
feeling.

A big factor in this uneasy feeling is that they're built for large groups of
people. Mall hallways are meant to support crowds of people moving
quickly in all directions, popping in and out of shops. When you find them
empty, or with a single person in them, the spaces feel enormous.

#+caption: An empty mall concourse at night. Photo by [[https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Interior_of_City-Center_empty_of_people_on_a_night_in_January_2025.jpg][JIP]], [[https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/][CC BY-SA 4.0]]
#+attr_html: :alt Empty shopping mall concourse at night, lit shop windows along one side, a juice bar along the other, and no people anywhere in the frame
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20260913-nowhere-specifically/mall.webp]]

These places aren't built to belong anywhere. A hotel hallway in one city looks
like a hotel hallway in any other city, because neither one was meant to tell
you where you are. The anthropologist Marc Augé called these [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-place][non-places]]:
motorways, hotel rooms, airports, malls. A real place gives you an identity and
a reason to be there. A non-place gives you neither. When you stop in one,
there's nothing in the room to tell you where you are, and the uneasy feeling
grows.

#+caption: A hotel corridor in Butte, Montana. Photo by [[https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Finlen_Hotel_hallway,_Butte_America,_January_2020_01.jpg][Montanabw]], [[https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/][CC BY-SA 4.0]]
#+attr_html: :alt Empty hotel corridor with dark patterned carpet, pale green walls, globe pendant lights, and doorways receding into the distance
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20260913-nowhere-specifically/hotel.webp]]

Part of the strangeness is that you recognize these rooms without ever having
been in them. The drop ceilings, fluorescent lights, and commercial carpet
are the same in thousands of buildings, so any one of them looks like all the
others you've walked through. You end up with a place you can recognize but
never identify. This is also how dreams can work, which is why people so often
describe these images as dreamlike.

The missing people are the focal point of the experience. People have described
these spaces as an uncanny valley of architecture: familiar enough to read as
normal, empty enough to read as wrong. A room built for hundreds with nobody in
it suggests that something happened to everyone else, or that you're the only
one left. Nothing in the room says that, but you feel it anyway.

Most of these interiors come from the 80s, 90s, and early 2000s, which means
they're the childhood environments of the people making and sharing the images.
The mall, the school hallway, the motel pool are all places you spent time in
before you had any say about where you went. Then COVID emptied real public
space for everyone at once, and the whole thing stopped being a niche
interest.

* The Backrooms

The best known version of all this is the [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Backrooms][Backrooms]]. In May 2019, someone on
4chan's paranormal board asked for images that "just feel off," and one of the
replies was a photo of a room with pale yellow walls, fluorescent lights, and a
tilted camera angle. Another user wrote a caption about noclipping out of bounds
in real life and ending up there. That was the whole thing, and it worked: a
room built for people, with no people, that you can recognize and can't place.

#+caption: The original backrooms photo, released into the public domain by Bob Mazza ([[https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HobbyTown_USA_Oshkosh_interior_under_construction_2002_%28The_Backrooms%29.jpg][CC0]])
#+attr_html: :alt Empty second-floor room with pale yellow patterned wallpaper, tan carpet, and rows of fluorescent lights, photographed during renovation
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20260913-nowhere-specifically/backrooms.webp]]

From there, it has grown exponentially. People added levels, then rules, then
monsters. Kane Parsons' YouTube shorts in early 2022 turned it into a full
horror franchise and took it mainstream.

Something got lost in that. The original photo is unsettling because nothing is
there. Once you put a creature in the room, the emptiness isn't doing the work
anymore; the monster is. It becomes a normal horror story that happens to be set
in a hallway, and the specific feeling that made anyone care about the photo in
the first place is gone.

* Liminal Is the Wrong Word

That brings up the word itself. [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liminality][Liminal]] comes from the Latin =limen=, meaning
threshold. The anthropologist [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_van_Gennep][Arnold van Gennep]] used it in 1909 to describe the
middle stage of a rite of passage: you're separated from your old role, you
spend time in between, and then you're brought back in with a new one. [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Turner][Victor
Turner]] picked the idea up in the 1960s and it spread from there.

The important part is that liminality describes a person, not a room. It's a
state you're in while you're changing from one thing into another, and it ends
when the change is done. A graduation is liminal. A funeral is liminal. A
hallway isn't.

So the spaces in these photos aren't liminal. They're non-places, and the
feeling you get standing in one is the absence of the crowd they were built for.
Calling that liminal borrows a word for something that was never about rooms at
all.

* Oshkosh, Wisconsin

In 2024, people finally [[https://boingboing.net/2024/05/30/original-backrooms-photo-of-yellowing-office-dungeon-finally-identified-as-oshkosh-hobbytown.html][tracked down]] where the original Backrooms photo was
taken. It's the second floor of [[https://pbswisconsin.org/article/how-a-furniture-store-in-oshkosh-became-a-portal-to-another-dimension/][a building on Oregon Street]]. That address held
Rohner's Furniture until 1994, and HobbyTown bought it in 2002 and started
remodeling. Someone photographed the empty upstairs that June and posted it to
the store's renovation blog the following year.

That's it. The most reproduced image of dread on the internet is a hobby shop's
construction photo. Nobody was trapped there and nothing was wrong with the
room. It was a store that wasn't finished yet. The building is a hobby shop
again today, and the spot where the photo was taken is now a carpet racetrack
for radio controlled cars.

Nothing in that photo was between one thing and another except the store. The
room was never liminal. You were.
