* Overview

The Gateway Arch sits on the St. Louis riverfront as a monument to westward
expansion. The site started as the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial in
1935, and Eero Saarinen's design won the competition for it in 1947. The arch is
an inverted catenary curve, 630 feet tall and 630 feet wide at the base, built
from stainless steel over concrete. Construction ran from 1963 to 1965, and the
tram to the top opened in 1967. In 2018 the memorial was renamed Gateway Arch
National Park, which made it the smallest national park in the country.

* The Experience

I was in St. Louis for work, and the arch was a short walk from the office, so I
went over after work one evening. The walk through the park is the best part of
getting there: green lawns, trees, and old buildings on every side of the arch.
During the winter season, the park is usually decorated with festive lights as
well.

#+caption: The Gateway Arch rising above the underground Visitor Center entrance
#+attr_html: :alt Visitors walking across the plaza toward the curved glass entrance of the Gateway Arch National Park Visitor Center with the full stainless steel arch rising against a blue sky with scattered white clouds
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261123-detour-002-gateway-arch/entrance.webp]]

The Visitor Center is underground, under the lawn between the legs of the arch,
which I thought was a great way to keep the grounds open. Inside there are
museum areas that I'd love to go through next time. This time I was racing the
clock to catch one of the last tram rides of the day, so I walked straight past
them. I'd love to visit again in the future and experience all the other aspects
of the park.

#+caption: Looking up along the stainless steel curve from the base of the Arch
#+attr_html: :alt Low-angle view from the grassy park grounds looking steeply upward along one reflective stainless steel leg of the Gateway Arch curving overhead against a bright blue sky, with river bridges visible in the background
[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261123-detour-002-gateway-arch/looking-up.webp]]

The tram up is a string of small pods, each with five seats, and they are not
built for someone my size. I squeezed in as the last passenger in my car, and an
older woman across from me pointed out that I was too big for it. She was
correct. Even by myself, it would've been uncomfortable, but putting 5 people in
there together is more uncomfortable than the smallest airplace seat I've been
in.

Luckily, the tram ride is only a few minutes long to the top and the windows let
you see the extensive stairwells and internals of the arch as you climb.

* Views from the Top

When you reach the top, the observation deck is a narrow, low room with small
slanted windows on both sides. For some reason, I have a thing for views from up
high, and this one did not disappoint.

[[https://img.cleberg.net/blog/20261123-detour-002-gateway-arch/630ft-sign.webp]]

#+caption: Classic triangular Gateway Arch sign beside a viewing window
#+attr_html: :alt Triangular sign inside the observation deck reading 192 M, 630 Feet, Gateway Arch St. Louis with an arch outline and National Park Service emblem beside a narrow viewing window overlooking the Old Courthouse

The west windows look over the park, the Old Courthouse, Busch Stadium, and
downtown St. Louis.

You're able to see all the way west to a surprising extent. In the photo below,
you can see a cluster of taller buildings far out - that's Clayton, a district
approximately 8 miles west of the Arch. At this height, the horizon should
extend out as far as 50 miles, which would include parts of the St. Charles
County side on a clear day. This photo was in July, so you can see the effects
of haze clouding the horizon beyond Clayton.

The east windows look across the Mississippi into Illinois, with the arch's

If you lean into a window, you can see straight down to both legs in the grass.

#+caption: Looking straight down at the Arch's legs and shadow
#+attr_html: :alt Steep downward view from the top of the Gateway Arch showing both stainless steel legs anchored in the grassy park grounds below and the full curved shadow of the Arch stretching across the riverfront into the Mississippi River

All in all, it's a great stop and definitely worth the time if you're near St.
Louis. I certainly wouldn't make a trip /just/ for this park, but it's pretty cool
regardless.

* Going

The arch is in downtown St. Louis, right off I-70, I-55, I-64 and I-44. If
you're in town, it's an easy stop. Get there with enough time for the museum
before your tram, which is the part I'll be going back for.
